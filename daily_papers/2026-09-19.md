## Ranked papers

### 1. **Limits of Confidence in Diffusion**

**Verdict:** must read direct diffusion/evaluation paper.

Why it matters: This is the cleanest paper in the batch because it attacks a deep habit in discrete diffusion: writing several positions at once based on per-position confidence. The paper shows that a parallel write matches the training distribution only when the written positions are conditionally independent given the frozen tokens. Per-position marginals cannot certify that condition. On the ScanAndAdd synthetic distribution, confidence-ordered remasking reaches perfect-looking per-sample correctness while the generated token distribution remains 29x the sampling-noise floor in total variation. The transferable lesson is sharp: a sampler can look correct while quietly destroying joint structure.

### 2. **Don't Mask the Environment: Observation Supervision Changes How Agents Explore Under RL**

**Verdict:** highly relevant direct RL/agent-training paper.

Why it matters: This paper tests a convention that usually passes unnoticed: SFT for agents predicts action tokens but masks environment observations, even though the trajectory already contains both. ActObs simply includes observation tokens in the SFT loss, so the model learns to predict consequences it will never emit at deployment. After GRPO, Qwen3-4B ActObs beats action-only SFT at every Terminal-Bench sampling budget and improves aider-polyglot pass@1 by 4.2 points. The mechanism claim is useful: observation supervision prevents one-sided action specialization, preserves consequence prediction, and leaves more entropy for RL exploration.

### 3. **Prediction-Powered Smoothing and Validation for Disaggregated AI Evaluation**

**Verdict:** highly relevant adjacent evaluation/statistics paper.

Why it matters: This paper is useful because it treats AI evaluation as a finite-population sampling problem instead of pretending a headline average is enough. It combines prediction-powered estimators with Fay-Herriot style small-area smoothing, then adds a design-based cross-validation score to choose among direct and smoothed estimators. On Open LLM Leaderboard task domains, PP-TS gets the best RMSE under all auxiliaries, dropping from 0.082 for HT to 0.059 with historical difficulty while keeping coverage near nominal. For deployed traffic-style evaluation, DB-CV selects the same best candidate ordering as the hidden oracle in the sample shown. The lesson is that disaggregated evaluation needs sampling design and model validation, not just an LLM judge.

### 4. **SplashSplat: Reconstructing Splashing Liquids from Real-World Multi-View Videos**

**Verdict:** useful direct 3D/dynamic-reconstruction paper.

Why it matters: SplashSplat is not a world model paper, but it has the right engineering taste: impose physical structure only where observations constrain it. The paper introduces a seven-camera, 4K, 60 fps benchmark of 20 real splashing-liquid scenes and reconstructs them by fusing per-frame masks into SDFs, fitting coarse level-set velocity fields, and advecting Lagrangian carriers that decode local Gaussians. On the real benchmark it reaches 20.13 PSNR / 0.9570 SSIM / 0.0806 LPIPS, beating 4D-Scaffold-GS at 18.92 / 0.9552 / 0.0898 while using less training time and memory. The caveat is that the dynamics are kinematic and visually constrained, not a complete physical state.

### 5. **Video DeltaNet: A Video-Native Hybrid Attention for Livestream Video Generation**

**Verdict:** useful direct video-generation systems paper.

Why it matters: Video DeltaNet is the best lighter recommendation today. It replaces dense video-to-video attention with a hybrid of local Softmax attention and bidirectional linear memory, updating the linear branch once per frame with spatial tokens. The claim is not just architectural tidiness: with eight-step distillation and an optimized serving stack, VDN-H3 completes DiT denoising for a 14.3 second 768p video in 6.70 seconds on eight NVIDIA B200 GPUs, reported as a 14.5x speedup over a 50-step dense H3 baseline. This is more systems-heavy than concept-heavy, but the video-native memory update is a real design point.

## Most relevant to cabbageland

**Most relevant today:** **Limits of Confidence in Diffusion**

It matters because cabbageland keeps running into the same hidden failure: local confidence is not joint state. The paper gives that failure a theorem, a toy distribution where the full truth is computable, and an evaluation result where correctness is perfect but distributional structure is wrong. That transfers beyond discrete diffusion: any system that commits pieces independently from marginal confidence needs a way to know when dependencies still matter.

## Novelty / framing / baseline impact

**Limits of Confidence** matters because it separates per-position confidence from joint correctness and shows why confidence-ordered parallel writes can be distributionally wrong even with exact conditionals.

**ActObs** matters because it changes only the SFT loss mask, yet shifts later RL exploration by preserving consequence modeling.

**Prediction-Powered Smoothing** matters because it makes disaggregated AI evaluation a sampling-and-validation problem rather than a leaderboard summarization problem.

**SplashSplat** matters because it constrains dynamic reconstruction with observed interface geometry and kinematic transport instead of pretending unconstrained dynamic Gaussians have learned fluid motion.

**Video DeltaNet** matters because it gives video diffusion a frame-level linear memory branch instead of importing language-model linear attention naively.

## One-paragraph takeaway

The September 19 pattern is that the easy summary is often the wrong object. Per-position confidence does not summarize a joint distribution. Action-token loss does not summarize an interactive trajectory. A headline benchmark score does not summarize domain performance. Photometric fit does not summarize liquid motion. Dense attention does not summarize the right computation for long video. The useful move across the batch is to expose the missing structure explicitly, then test whether the shortcut still holds.
