# Daily Paper Digest - 2026-07-15

## Theme

Today's strongest papers all attack the same bad habit: mistaking a good score for the right internal state. **When Does Reward Teach State?** shows that an RL agent can earn decent reward while never learning the latent task state you actually wanted. **GRID** does the systems version by compiling role and schema constraints into the decoding language instead of trusting prompt obedience. **Repairing Shape-Prior Shortcuts in Long-Range Single-Shot Fringe Projection Profilometry** forces a vision model through phase and calibration so it cannot free-ride on silhouettes. **How Query Visibility Changes KV-Cache Compression Rankings** shows that a cache compressor can look strong when it is allowed to peek at the very query it is supposed to serve later. **Contrastive Joint-Embedding Prediction for Representation Learning in Structural MRI** is the quieter representation-learning version: if you want both local structure and global discriminability, put both pressures into the objective.

## Ranked papers

### 1. **When Does Reward Teach State? A Hidden-Automaton Instrument and the Group-Language Boundary**

**Verdict:** must read for agent evaluation, RL, and latent-state claims.

Why it matters: This paper takes an argument that is usually mushy, "did the agent understand the task or just learn a shortcut?", and turns it into an exact measurement problem. In its hidden-automaton control testbed, weak on-policy RL can achieve reward while the latent-state probe stays at chance, PPO partially recovers state, and permutation structure predicts `89` of `103` perception gaps at `0.86` precision. The most valuable output is not a new agent but a sharper vocabulary: reward can hide a **perception gap** or a **planning gap**, and those are different failures.

### 2. **GRID: Grammar-Railed Decoding for Enterprise SQL Generation**

**Verdict:** must read systems paper with strong direct relevance.

Why it matters: GRID is what "structured decoding" looks like when the authors actually care about invariants instead of demo vibes. The core move is to key exact next-token masks on live parser configuration plus lexer state, then compile role policy and schema lexicons into the language itself. On Spider, the mask is worth `+13` execution-accuracy points at `0.5B`, while a `7B` model gets from `53.7%` EX with mask-only to `55.2%` with one checker-guided repair pass. The latency story is also real: `3.6` to `6.7` microsecond median masks with explicit honesty about cold tails and out-of-scope failures.

### 3. **Repairing Shape-Prior Shortcuts in Long-Range Single-Shot Fringe Projection Profilometry**

**Verdict:** preserve-worthy adjacent inspiration with high direct relevance.

Why it matters: This is the cleanest recent example I have seen of fixing a shortcut by changing the representation rather than adding more loss glitter. Instead of regressing depth directly, PhiCalNet predicts wrapped phase, unwraps with fringe order, and maps to depth through a fixed differentiable calibration layer. That architectural constraint drops object MAE from `14.54 mm` to `4.46 mm`, confines the residual to `0.103%` of pixels at the wrap boundary, and makes a physics-in-the-loss baseline look properly decorative.

### 4. **How Query Visibility Changes KV-Cache Compression Rankings: A Matched-Budget Audit**

**Verdict:** strong direct-relevance audit paper.

Why it matters: The paper fixes a dumb but common evaluation bug: compressing a cache after the query is already visible and then pretending that says something about reusable context compression. Under the proper query-agnostic protocol, only KeyDiff consistently beats the best trivial baseline across `31/36` RULER cells, while SnapKV averages below a simple "keep the start and recent window" rule at `-0.066`. It also catches two broader review-pathology issues: backend changes can move scores more than the methods do, and benchmark token lengths can silently invalidate whole subtasks.

### 5. **Contrastive Joint-Embedding Prediction for Representation Learning in Structural MRI**

**Verdict:** useful adjacent inspiration and honest lighter recommendation.

Why it matters: COJEPA is not a grand medical foundation-model paper, but it does contain one reusable design lesson: predictive and contrastive pressures are not interchangeable, and combining them can give better global discrimination without obviously wrecking local structure. On structural MRI, the combined objective gets the best monozygotic twin retrieval at `0.84` rank-1 and the best finetuned age MAE at `2.55` years, while staying competitive on tumor segmentation. I kept it fifth because the dataset is small by SSL standards and the gains are modest rather than field-redefining, but the objective-level lesson is real.

## Most relevant to cabbageland

**Most relevant today:** When Does Reward Teach State?

The main steal is the exact separation between task success and task-state recovery. If an agent succeeds, that does not tell you whether it internally tracks the right latent state, whether it merely latched onto a shortcut, or whether it learned a state representation but fails to use it. This paper gives a concrete instrument for separating those cases. That is directly useful for long-horizon agents, tool-use policies, workflow models, and any "world model" claim that currently rests on downstream reward plus vibes.

**GRID** supplies the systems complement: if a constraint matters, compile it into the interface instead of hoping the model remembers it. **PhiCalNet** supplies the representation complement: if a shortcut is too available, change the output space so the shortcut stops being a cheap solution. **The KV-cache audit** supplies the evaluation complement: match the protocol to the deployment story, or the leaderboard is lying. **COJEPA** adds the quieter objective-design lesson: if you want both specificity and predictivity, train for both.

## Novelty / framing / baseline impact

**When Does Reward Teach State?** is strong because it upgrades "did the agent really understand the task?" from a vibes question into a measurable one. The exact latent state and exact optimal value make it possible to separate reward, representational recovery, and action use. The pre-registered PPO control is also a good sign: the paper explicitly overturns its own too-strong initial story that sparse-reward RL simply cannot install state. Caveat: the main evidence is still on synthetic hidden-automaton tasks, with only a preliminary LLM-agent appendix for external validity.

**GRID** is strong because it solves the right subproblem. It does not promise semantic omniscience; it promises exact grammar, role, and schema validity with measured hot-path cost and an explicit checker for the remaining residue. The useful novelty is not "grammar constraints for SQL" in the abstract, but configuration-keyed masking plus policy compiled into the language. Caveat: column-level RBAC remains post-parse, the guarantees are limited to LALR(1)-parsable languages, and the cold specialization tails are real even if honestly documented.

**Repairing Shape-Prior Shortcuts in Long-Range Single-Shot Fringe Projection Profilometry** is strong because it isolates the causal variable. The paper does not just say "add physics" and hope. It shows that a PINN-style soft physics loss does not remove the shortcut, while a phase-intermediate architecture with fixed calibration does. Caveat: the benchmark is synthetic and the best results depend on oracle fringe order, so this is a sharp upper-bound architecture study rather than a complete field-ready pipeline.

**How Query Visibility Changes KV-Cache Compression Rankings** is strong because it changes one variable and then retracts claims when confounds appear. That alone already beats a lot of systems evaluation. The paper's framing should affect how people review cache-compression work: if the deployment story is reuse, then query-aware compression is a different task. Caveat: the evidence is still limited to three open 7-9B models, and the LongBench check shows that KeyDiff keeps its validity but loses its monopoly on natural text.

**Contrastive Joint-Embedding Prediction for Representation Learning in Structural MRI** is useful because it says something narrower and more believable than "we trained a foundation model." The combination of JEPA-style local prediction with a weak contrastive term improves subject-level discrimination and age transfer while leaving segmentation roughly intact. Caveat: the pretraining pool is only `2286` healthy subjects, scanner-domain gaps remain visible, and the paper does not yet establish broad clinical robustness.

## One-paragraph takeaway

The best papers today all punish proxy success. Reward can go up while latent-state learning stays absent. SQL generation can look compliant only if the policy and schema live inside the decoding language, not just around it. Single-shot 3D reconstruction can look good while cheating off outlines unless the architecture forces a phase-like state. KV-cache compressors can look strong when they are allowed to see the question they are supposed to support later. Even the MRI paper lands in the same neighborhood: if you want a representation to be both locally predictive and globally specific, say so in the objective. Same lesson across five papers: stop inferring the right internal computation from a flattering headline metric.
