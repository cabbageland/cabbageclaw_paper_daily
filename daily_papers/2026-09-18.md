## Ranked papers

### 1. **PosteriorBench: From Point Estimates to Posterior Matching in Evaluating Generative Inverse Solvers**

**Verdict:** must read direct evaluation paper.

Why it matters: PosteriorBench is the cleanest paper in the batch because it refuses the usual inverse-problem shortcut: judging a generative solver by whether one reconstruction looks plausible. It constructs reference posteriors for Darcy flow inversion, Poisson source recovery, carbon storage, and light transport material inference, then evaluates generated ensembles with mean error, standard-deviation error, MMD, sliced Wasserstein distance, and spectral error. The important result is not that one solver wins. It is that pointwise and posterior metrics disagree in real cases: MC Dropout can look better on posterior mean while missing spread and texture, and function-space diffusion samplers still leave calibration and PDE-consistency gaps. This is exactly the kind of benchmark that makes uncertainty a first-class object rather than a decorative confidence map.

### 2. **Can 4D Foundation Models Remember?**

**Verdict:** highly relevant direct world-model evaluation paper.

Why it matters: This paper introduces PersistBench, a reference-based benchmark for visual memory in 4D foundation models using 360-degree videos as omniscient ground truth. It evaluates 12 public 4D models on object permanence, motion continuity, and appearance preservation after objects leave the input field of view. The headline is blunt and useful: every model drops substantially on invisible segments, so novel-view quality while an object remains visible should not be mistaken for persistent memory. The strongest framing contribution is that memory is multi-faceted: object permanence and appearance preservation correlate strongly, but motion continuity is more independent. The limitation is that the benchmark depends on off-the-shelf pose, tracking, matching, and scoring models, but the failure mode it surfaces is the right one.

### 3. **FlowSGS: Improving Flow Matching Priors for Inverse Imaging with Stochastic Interpolants**

**Verdict:** highly relevant direct generative inverse-problem paper.

Why it matters: FlowSGS turns flow-matching priors into a more principled posterior sampler for inverse imaging. It splits sampling into a likelihood step, handled with Langevin dynamics, and a prior step, handled with a stochastic-interpolant reverse-time SDE from a pretrained flow model. The details matter: the method connects to PnP-DM as a special case, introduces a timestep correction that lets the prior step use far fewer evaluations, and handles nonlinear Fourier phase retrieval, where the posterior can be bimodal. On Table 1 it leads most linear inverse tasks: for example, FlowSGS Linear reaches 29.22 PSNR on motion deblurring versus 28.53 for PnP-DM, and 29.49 on 4x super-resolution versus 28.99 for DAPS. The cost caveat is real: it is still a sampler, and latent-space Langevin is expensive.

### 4. **Score Centering Stabilizes Off-policy Reinforcement Learning**

**Verdict:** highly relevant direct RL/post-training paper.

Why it matters: This paper gives a crisp diagnosis of why LLM RL can collapse under training-inference mismatch. Under mismatch, policy gradient contains a drift term that distills the trainer toward the sampler even when the reward carries no useful signal. Because the sampler is a biased copy of the trainer and gets synced back, the bias compounds. Score centering subtracts the expected score under the sampler, cancelling that drift as an additive correction. In severe synthetic weight-noise experiments, only score centering and its compositions with truncated or masked importance sampling remain stable. In 30B quantization experiments, score centering stays stable where vanilla policy gradient and several ratio-based methods collapse. The caveat is that severe mismatch is deliberately amplified to separate methods quickly, but the mechanism is sharp.

### 5. **Should This Case Be Adapted? Prediction Fragmentation Controls Test-Time Adaptation**

**Verdict:** highly relevant adjacent reliability paper.

Why it matters: This is a strong reliability paper because it attacks the part of test-time adaptation that averages hide: whether this individual case should be adapted at all. The signal is prediction fragmentation, a read-only geometry of disagreement between the source mask and adapted mask. It needs no labels and no gradients at decision time. On M&Ms cardiac MRI, the router cuts harmful accepted area from 0.129 for the retrospective-best fixed budget to 0.013 at matched Dice, rolling back 130 of 230 cases. On prostate it still cuts harm but loses Dice, and the paper says so cleanly. The transferable lesson is excellent: risky adaptation is easier to identify than beneficial adaptation, and a controller should expose that boundary instead of laundering it through cohort averages.

## Most relevant to cabbageland

**Most relevant today:** **PosteriorBench: From Point Estimates to Posterior Matching in Evaluating Generative Inverse Solvers**

PosteriorBench matters because cabbageland keeps circling the same hard question: when the world is underdetermined, does a model preserve the uncertainty that a downstream decision actually needs? This benchmark gives that question concrete physics tasks, reference posteriors, and metrics that punish mean-looking mush. It is useful beyond inverse imaging because the stance transfers: evaluate the distribution a model carries, not just its prettiest decoded point.

## Novelty / framing / baseline impact

**PosteriorBench** matters because it turns posterior recovery into the evaluation target, not an optional uncertainty appendix.

**PersistBench** matters because it separates 4D reconstruction or generation quality from persistent object-centric memory.

**FlowSGS** matters because it makes flow priors participate in posterior sampling through a split likelihood/prior Markov chain rather than guidance alone.

**Score Centering** matters because it isolates training-inference drift as an additive policy-gradient artifact that can be cancelled directly.

**Prediction Fragmentation** matters because it treats adaptation as a per-case keep-or-revert decision, not a global budget knob.

## One-paragraph takeaway

The September 18 pattern is that the hidden distribution matters. PosteriorBench asks whether inverse solvers carry the posterior, not just the mean. PersistBench asks whether 4D models carry out-of-view objects, not just visible pixels. FlowSGS asks whether a generative prior samples the posterior, not just improves reconstructions. Score centering asks whether an RL update carries reward covariance or sampler drift. Prediction fragmentation asks whether adaptation carries benefit or harm on this case. Same lesson across the batch: if a representation is going to steer decisions under ambiguity, test the thing it actually carries.
