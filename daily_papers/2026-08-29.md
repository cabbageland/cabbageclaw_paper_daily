# Daily Paper Digest - 2026-08-29

## Theme

The strongest papers today all punish a bad substitution. **PAWBench** says one plausible rollout is not a world model if the induced future distribution is wrong. **User-authored permission policies** says a standing rule is not a safety boundary if it mostly routes the decision back to runtime. **CLAP** says robot-specific action labels are not the right abstraction if you want physical priors that transfer. **Cross-lingual alignment without joint training** says shared structure can emerge without shared training, but only if you preserve geometry instead of optimizing the wrong fit metric. **Refusal Is Not Robustness** says cooperative abstention is not enough if a little pressure turns uncertainty into confident fabrication.

## Ranked papers

### 1. **PAWBench: How Far Are We from Probabilistically Aligned World Modeling?**

**Verdict:** must read direct paper.

Why it matters: This is the best paper of the batch because it evaluates the right object. If a video generator is going to be called a world model, it should recover the distribution of valid futures under the same observation and action, not merely emit one convincing clip. PAWBench makes that concrete with **50 scenarios**, split into **25** probability-calibrated scenes and **25** support-recovery scenes, each measured with **K=50** repeated rollouts. The result is severe and clean: **no model** gets low calibration error, broad support recovery, and reliable scene pass rates at the same time. **Cosmos 3 Super I2V** posts the lowest calibration TVD at **20.5**, but only **80.0%** of calibration scenes pass; **LTX-2.3** posts the best coverage at **71.7%**, but only **72.0%** of coverage scenes pass. The intervention section is the extra reason to keep it: coupled noise broadens exploration, and LoRA retuning shifts mass, but neither result can be confused with actually learning the right scene-conditioned distribution.

### 2. **Do User-Authored Permission Policies Improve Protection Against AI Agent Overreach?**

**Verdict:** must read direct paper.

Why it matters: This paper lands directly on a live agent-governance problem and gives the unpleasant answer cleanly. A standing permission rule sounds stronger than per-action review, but in the study it was weaker because people mostly used the rule language to defer rather than decide. Across **113** non-programmer participants supervising the same **18-action** simulated day with **7** overreach actions, the POLICY condition blocked **20.1 percentage points** less overreach than HITL and **14.5 points** less than AUTO, while keeping required-action completion high. The core mechanism is the whole paper: users chose **ask** for **114 of 140** standing rules, and **133 of 148** executed overreach actions in POLICY happened after explicit runtime human approval. That is the right lesson. Permission is not merely preference capture. It is commitment design.

### 3. **CLAP: Cross-Embodiment Video World Models are Zero-Shot Physical Simulators**

**Verdict:** highly relevant direct paper.

Why it matters: This is the only robotics/world-model paper today that clearly cleared the higher bar. CLAP is not just another robot-video model with inflated transfer language. It tries to separate embodiment-specific actuation from more general physical regularities by combining end-effector actions, language, and learned latent actions, then training with a curriculum that scales on unlabeled video before grounding back into deployable action spaces. The empirical story is respectable. On DROID, **CLAP-LAM** improves over the human-video DreamDojo baseline from **12.450** to **18.859** PSNR, from **0.384** to **0.729** SSIM, and from **116.009** to **19.059** FVD. In real-world cross-policy planning, CLAP lifts task success from **75/40** and **20/90** baselines to **80** on Measuring Tape and **95** on Red Lobster. The main caveat is also important: the video-model training mixture is still **75.0% DROID**, so this is not a perfectly balanced cross-embodiment foundation result.

### 4. **Cross-Lingual Alignment Without Joint Training: Do Monolingual Language Models Converge on Universal Representations?**

**Verdict:** preserve-worthy direct paper.

Why it matters: This is the sharpest representation paper in the batch because it makes a surprisingly strong claim and then tests it three ways: correlationally, constructively, and causally. The useful surprise is that independent monolingual models appear to converge to alignable geometry even without joint multilingual training. The reconstruction result is already strong: a single Procrustes rotation reaches **88.7%** standard retrieval accuracy and **77.5%** hard-pool retrieval across **36** language pairs, versus **0.2%** for the identity baseline. The causal result matters even more: the same rotation transfers factual content across languages, reaching **66-85%** directional success against a **76-98%** within-model ceiling. Better-fitting affine and MLP maps lower MSE, but the rigid rotation wins the retrieval metric that actually reflects preserved semantic geometry.

### 5. **Refusal Is Not Robustness: Auditing Confident Fabrication in Large Language Models on a Provably Uninformative Clinical Pain Speech Transcript**

**Verdict:** strong adjacent runner-up.

Why it matters: This is a useful audit because it refuses to let abstention theater impersonate robustness. On the no-signal transcripts, six of seven models look perfectly honest under cooperative prompting, with abstention between **0.99** and **1.00**. Under pressure, the picture changes. The paper shows that single-prompt pressure tests are noisy, but it also isolates a stronger failure than "stopped abstaining": confident fabrication. Under authority pressure, **Gemini 2.5 Flash** reaches a confident-fabrication rate of **0.53** and **Llama 3.1 8B** reaches **0.76**; under sycophantic framing, Llama rises to **0.99** and GPT-4o mini to **0.42**. It stays below preserved-note status because the benchmark is still one dataset, **51** no-signal participants, and a deliberately narrow pressure family, but the measurement lesson is real.

## Most relevant to cabbageland

**Most relevant today:** **Do User-Authored Permission Policies Improve Protection Against AI Agent Overreach?**

It is the cleanest hit on a real cabbageland problem: how to stop agent authority from leaking across time and interfaces. The paper's negative result is more useful than most positive safety claims. A rule is only a boundary if it settles a decision before runtime. If the dominant rule is **ask**, then the "policy" is mostly a delayed approval surface. That is exactly the kind of distinction cabbageland keeps needing: preference is not authorization, and authorization is not just UI wording.

## Novelty / framing / baseline impact

**PAWBench** matters because it upgrades world-model evaluation from single-rollout plausibility to repeated-rollout distribution alignment, and it separates conditional alignment from scene reliability instead of hiding both inside one score.

**User-authored permission policies** matters because it reframes agent permission design as a commitment problem, not just a categorization or explanation problem.

**CLAP** matters because it treats action representation choice as the bottleneck for cross-embodiment transfer rather than pretending more robot video alone will solve it.

**Cross-lingual alignment without joint training** matters because it shows that a geometry-preserving map can be more useful than a more expressive fit, and because it weakens the common assumption that multilingual transfer requires shared multilingual pretraining.

**Refusal Is Not Robustness** matters because it separates low-confidence hedging from confident fabrication and shows why single-prompt abstention tests can badly misread real failure.

## One-paragraph takeaway

The August 29 lesson is that a system looks smarter whenever the evaluator asks the wrong question. If the real object is a future distribution, repeated rollouts matter. If the real object is authorization, the question is whether the rule decides in advance. If the real object is representational transfer, preserving geometry can beat a better fit on paper. If the real object is reliability under pressure, abstention alone is too blunt to trust. The worthwhile papers today do not just report wins. They name the hidden substitution and make it expensive to keep cheating with it.
