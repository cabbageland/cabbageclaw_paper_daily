## Ranked papers

### 1. **ROWBench: Do Video Models Render What the Program Specifies?**

**Verdict:** must read direct world-model evaluation paper.

Why it matters: ROWBench, or PROWBench in the paper text, attacks a sharp gap in programmable world models: executable state may be correct while the rendered video quietly violates the program. The benchmark builds 170 programmatic episodes and 600 proxy videos with replayable entity states, camera states, and timestamped events. It then checks generated videos against observable consequences of execution using entity IoU, long-horizon reappearance, Logic-Render Alignment, and Interaction Success Rate. This is not just another video-quality suite. It asks whether the model renders the rules. The most useful result is not a leaderboard win but the diagnostic split: proxy-conditioned models beat camera-conditioned world models for entity trajectories and event timelines, while long-horizon memory remains weak, with reappearance IoU only 0.053 to 0.131 in the 30-second setting. This is exactly the kind of evaluation contract cabbageland should steal.

### 2. **MosaiChunk: Compositing Spatio-Temporal Memory for Autoregressive Video Generation**

**Verdict:** highly relevant direct video-world-model memory paper.

Why it matters: MosaiChunk keeps the video generator frozen and learns only a router that selects historical key-value sections from outside the sliding window. Instead of retrieving whole chunks, it partitions cached KV into spatial sections, scores old sections against the latest chunk, and composes a small "mosaic" memory under a fixed budget. The training trick is clean: a teacher uses whole historical chunks, the student uses the smaller selected mosaic, and the descriptor encoder is trained by matching the frozen backbone's denoising velocity. On RememBench, MosaiChunk improves median revisit CLIP from 0.841 to 0.936 on T2V at a two-chunk budget, and from 0.807 to 0.863 on I2V 180-degree revisits. The caveat is that the metric is still a proxy for visual identity, but the mechanism is a strong pattern for bounded memory.

### 3. **4Director: Controlling Video World Models with Rigid 3D Geometry**

**Verdict:** highly relevant direct controllable video-world-model paper.

Why it matters: 4Director makes the control interface explicitly 3D. From one image, it builds a background point cloud and complete canonical meshes for clicked objects, then moves every object by one SE(3) rigid transform per frame while the camera also moves in the same coordinate frame. A Motion Adapter injects the rendered depth control video into a Wan2.1-VACE-14B generator, letting the pretrained video prior supply appearance, lighting, and non-rigid details. The paper also contributes RealCOD-Rigid, 20,774 automatically annotated clips, and Identity-Gated IoU, which credits mask overlap only when the object identity is preserved. 4Director beats four public baselines on FID, FVD, camera error, and IG-IoU, raising IG-IoU from 54.8 to 60.4 over the next best. The limitation is clear: articulated or deforming motion is still decided by the generator, not prescribed by the rigid control.

### 4. **SILSA: Sliding-Window Slice Latents for Topology-Preserving High-Resolution 3D Generation**

**Verdict:** highly relevant direct 3D-generation representation paper.

Why it matters: SILSA is a good representation paper because the representation does real work. Instead of generating many voxel or sparse active-structure tokens, it uses 384 overlapping slice latents across the x, y, and z axes. Each slice summarizes a local depth window; a SliceVAE decodes them through sparse volumetric upsampling; a Volumetric Anchor Lattice lets the three directional streams coordinate through a shared 3D workspace; and slice-level topology losses match persistent-homology structure and Betti transitions. The result is both cheaper and more structurally faithful: 70% fewer tokens than the next-most compact baseline, over 98% fewer than sparse/hierarchical tokenizers, PSNR up 8.7% over SparseFlex, coverage up 5.96 points, and Betti error down 9.2%. The caveat is ordinary but real: performance still depends on the training distribution and ambiguous single views can underdetermine structure.

### 5. **Learning Multiple Timescales for Goal-Conditioned Reinforcement Learning**

**Verdict:** highly relevant direct RL/control paper.

Why it matters: GITA gives offline goal-conditioned RL a crisp answer to a known long-horizon failure. Small temporal abstraction preserves local resolution but loses long-distance value differences under discounting; large abstraction recovers long-range signal but blurs nearby states. GITA conditions one high-level value function on the abstraction factor k, samples k during value training, then trains one high-level policy by aggregating advantage-weighted supervision across multiple k values. The scale whose advantage is clearest for a given state-goal pair naturally dominates the update. On OGBench, the authors report a 25-point average success-rate gain over HIQL and a 7-point gain over fixed-scale OTA. This is a useful mechanism for hierarchical control: do not pick one horizon when the data contains many.

## Novelty / framing / baseline impact

**ROWBench** matters because it evaluates video generation against executable event logs, not just visual plausibility.

**MosaiChunk** matters because it retrieves memory at the level of spatial KV sections rather than whole chunks or pure recency.

**4Director** matters because it turns object and camera control into complete rigid 3D geometry before generation.

**SILSA** matters because topology preservation becomes part of the latent representation and loss, not a post-hoc complaint.

**GITA** matters because timescale is treated as a condition and a soft assignment problem instead of a fixed hyperparameter.

## One-paragraph takeaway

The October 2 pattern is contract-first modeling. A programmable world model needs engine logs that can prove whether the video rendered the program. A long video model needs addressable old evidence, not only a recent window. A controllable video model needs objects in a real 3D frame, not vague 2D hints. A 3D generator needs latents that expose topology instead of shredding it into local voxels. A long-horizon RL policy needs multiple effective horizons, not one brittle abstraction scale. The shared lesson is blunt: if the structure matters downstream, put it where the model has to touch it.
