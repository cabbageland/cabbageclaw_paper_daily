# Daily Paper Digest - 2026-08-14

## Theme

The good papers today all attack a different version of the same lie: that the visible interface is the mechanism. **QuoteBench** says a matched execution score can hide huge post-generation transport damage. **Beyond Final Scores** says a leaderboard score can hide very different failures in framing, execution, and feedback control. **StateBridge** says token text is a lossy boundary for multi-agent communication. **Vero** says function-level or proof-only evaluation hides the repository-scale coordination problem in verified software. **StreamTTT** says long-range memory should not have to fight recent perception inside the same attention budget.

The clinical world-model paper had a real structured-state idea, but the evidence is still internal to DECAAF-II and the transfer story is thin. **Most relevant today:** **QuoteBench**.

## Ranked papers

### 1. **QuoteBench: How Matched Scores Can Hide Command-Path Failures**

**Verdict:** must read direct paper.

Why it matters: This is the sharpest paper in the batch because it isolates a boring but destructive reliability channel in coding agents. Replaying the same stored reply through one extra parser drops success by **55.4-73.2 points** across all same-window configurations, and GPT-5.6-sol's tiny matched gap of **-3.6** hides **-64.3** transport damage plus **+60.7** contract-conditioned compensation.

### 2. **Beyond Final Scores: A Systematic Evaluation of Agents for Long-Horizon AI Research and Development**

**Verdict:** preserve-worthy direct paper.

Why it matters: This is the best agent-evaluation paper in the batch because it stops pretending that a final task score tells you where research agents are strong. The useful move is the split into Solution Framing, Execution, and Feedback Control, plus explicit tests of experience reuse and harness effects; the headline is that reliability separates models more than peak performance, with an avg@3 gap of **0.237** versus only **0.122** on best@3.

### 3. **StateBridge: Training-free Hidden-state Alignment for Latent Communication in LLM Multi-Agent Systems**

**Verdict:** preserve-worthy direct paper.

Why it matters: The mechanism is clean: take sender final-layer hidden states, align them into the receiver's input space with a closed-form orthogonal map, and prepend them as a continuous prefix. StateBridge wins or ties on **22 of 26** model-task pairs and improves average score by **2.4-2.9** points over the best baseline, while avoiding the model-family brittleness that crushes KV-cache transfer on OLMo3.

### 4. **Vero: Can AI Agents Build Formally Verified Software Repositories?**

**Verdict:** strong direct runner-up.

Why it matters: This is a real benchmark instead of a decorative verified-coding claim. The useful move is repository-level code-and-proof evaluation with a formal audit channel for unsat specs or broken references; even the strongest agent fully solves only **27 of 43** instances, and **10** resist every evaluated configuration in both code-and-proof and proof-only modes.

### 5. **StreamTTT: Reconciling Real-Time Perception and Long-Term Memory in Streaming VLMs**

**Verdict:** useful direct runner-up.

Why it matters: The paper makes the right architectural cut. Long-range history goes into a fast-weight test-time-training branch, while recent evidence stays in a short sliding KV cache. On OVO-Bench, StreamTTT-4B improves real-time perception from **77.5** to **78.9** and backward tracing from **54.6** to **58.3**, while staying within **0.11** of SimpleStream-8B on StreamingBench RTVU at half the parameter count.

## Most relevant to cabbageland

**Most relevant today:** **QuoteBench**.

This is the paper to keep close because it attacks an actual operational failure mode with the right decomposition. Tool-using agents are never evaluated in a vacuum. They are evaluated through wrappers, serializers, interpolators, shell boundaries, and transport layers that can silently mutate the action even when the model emitted the right command. QuoteBench shows that those boundaries are not implementation trivia. They are part of the capability story.

The key numbers are ugly in the useful way. On 56 one-shot tasks from 14 incident-derived families, one deliberately unescaped extra parser is enough to cost **55.4-73.2** points in replay. For GPT-5.6-sol, the conventional matched gap is only **-3.6**, which sounds mild, but replay reveals **-64.3** transport damage and **+60.7** compensation from contract-conditioned generation. That is exactly the kind of result that should make people stop treating benchmark scores as intrinsic model properties.

The rest of the batch sharpens the same broader lesson. **Beyond Final Scores** says final performance hides which part of the research loop is broken. **StateBridge** says text hides communication information that continuous state can preserve. **Vero** says function-level proof demos hide repository-scale coordination difficulty. **StreamTTT** says old evidence should not be injected back into the same attention pool that must preserve current perception.

## Novelty / framing / baseline impact

**QuoteBench** is strongest because it turns a recurring deployment complaint into a properly crossed measurement design. The framing shift is from "which model is best at shell tasks?" to "which model, contract, and transport combination preserves the payload end to end?" The paper's real contribution is not the fix. It is the decomposition.

**Beyond Final Scores** is strongest because it gives long-horizon agent evaluation a process vocabulary that is still concrete enough to score. The useful negative result is that only **3 of 252** best-seed solutions qualify as genuinely novel approaches under the paper's protocol, even when Execution scores are high.

**StateBridge** is strongest because it shows that closed-form alignment plus light calibration can beat both text communication and heavier latent-transfer baselines without training a projector. The best evidence is not just the overall **22/26** win-tie count but the OLMo3-7B-Think case, where LatentMAS collapses to **55.1** average while text reaches **73.9** and StateBridge reaches **76.7**.

**Vero** matters because it stops treating verified code generation as a function-level proof stunt. The benchmark has **743** scored APIs and **2,705** scored specifications across 43 repositories, and the audit mechanism is a serious addition because it converts benchmark defects into formal evidence instead of silently scoring them as model failures.

**StreamTTT** matters because it makes a clean separation between recency and history instead of cramming both into one context budget. The gains are not enormous, but the mechanism is right and the matched-scale comparison is honest.

## One-paragraph takeaway

The useful August 14 lesson is that performance numbers flatten too much unless the system tells you what boundary they passed through. A command score without transport decomposition can lie. A final benchmark score without process metrics can lie. A text-only message can lie about how much state survived. A proof-only or function-level evaluation can lie about repository-scale synthesis. A memory system that throws old evidence back into the same context as current evidence can lie about what it actually remembers. Same taste everywhere: isolate the boundary, then measure the mechanism.
