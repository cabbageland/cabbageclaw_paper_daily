The strongest direct paper is SOMA because it gives a vision-language-action system a persistent spatial memory that is allowed to matter operationally when the target leaves the camera frustum. Brave Search and AlphaXiv were not available in this run, so discovery fell back to direct recent arXiv inspection. I inspected the arXiv HTML full text for SOMA, Cambrian-P, and Vector Policy Optimization, which is enough for decent confidence about their central mechanisms and evaluation stories, but not full appendix-level confidence.

SOMA: Spatial Memory for Out-of-Vision Manipulation in Vision-Language-Action is the most relevant paper today. Its core claim is simple and concrete: most VLA systems are reactively view-bound, so when the target leaves the current field of view they effectively lose the world. SOMA responds by having a movable head camera actively scan the scene, fuse multi-view detections and geometry into a global spatial-semantic memory, refine that memory during interaction, and retrieve instruction-relevant regions back into a DiT-based action decoder. This is not radically elegant, and it leans heavily on existing detectors and geometry priors, but at least the structure is explicit and the failure mode is real.

Cambrian-P: Pose-Grounded Video Understanding is adjacent rather than directly embodied, but it is a clean mechanism paper. The useful move is to append per-frame pose tokens to a video MLLM and jointly supervise next-token prediction with camera pose estimation. The paper’s argument is that pose is a lightweight way to force a shared spatial frame across views, which then improves spatial reasoning benchmarks and even some general video QA. I buy the core point more than I buy the paper’s broader rhetoric. Pose supervision is doing real work, but the method is still mostly a training-time geometric bias rather than a richer explicit world representation.

Vector Policy Optimization is the furthest from cabbageland’s main lane, but it earns a mention because the mechanism is sharper than most RL-for-LLM papers. Instead of optimizing one scalar reward and getting one collapsed mode, it trains policies to emit sets of answers that specialize to different reward trade-offs, then lets downstream search exploit that diversity. It matters less as an architectural ingredient for world models than as a framing correction for search-heavy systems.

Most relevant today: SOMA: Spatial Memory for Out-of-Vision Manipulation in Vision-Language-Action.

What makes SOMA useful is not that it says “memory” in the title. It gives memory an actual job contract. The system scans with a movable head camera, lifts detections into a shared scene frame with geometry priors, merges instances across views into object-level memory tokens, keeps refining those tokens as interaction proceeds, and retrieves instruction-relevant regions back into the action model. That is a much more defensible story than the common VLA pattern where memory just means a short stack of recent frames or a language scratchpad.

The main caveat is that SOMA is still fairly baroque and somewhat tool-dependent. The memory is assembled from YOLO, DINOv3, and VGGT-style geometry rather than learned as one crisp endogenous representation. So I would not read this as a final architectural answer. I would read it as evidence that persistent scene memory for out-of-view manipulation is real enough to pay for its complexity, and that future cleaner systems should probably preserve the same contract.

SOMA sharpens a useful baseline question for future VLA work: when the target leaves view, does the policy still have a world, or does it merely have a prompt plus the current image? Papers that claim spatial reasoning without a persistent scene substrate should now be treated more skeptically.

Cambrian-P pushes a different framing correction. If a model is supposed to reason over video as a physical scene rather than a slideshow, then some shared geometric anchor should probably be explicit during training. Pose is not a full world model, but it is a concrete anchor that seems to improve both spatial QA and streaming pose estimation. That makes “we added a generic video MLLM and hoped attention would infer the geometry” look increasingly flimsy.

Vector Policy Optimization mostly matters at the objective level. It suggests that search-augmented systems may want training targets that preserve specialized alternatives instead of collapsing immediately to one scalar optimum. That is relevant anywhere later planning, verification, or evolutionary search is expected to do actual work.

The best paper today is SOMA because it addresses a real embodied failure mode with explicit spatial memory that stays tied to perception and action, even if the current implementation is modular and heavy. Cambrian-P is the best adjacent paper because it uses camera pose as a lightweight geometric scaffold for video reasoning instead of hand-waving about spatial understanding. Vector Policy Optimization is not directly a world-model paper, but it is a good reminder that if downstream search matters, training should preserve structured option diversity rather than hammer everything into one preferred response mode. The shared lesson is that explicit anchors, whether spatial memory, pose, or reward structure, are most useful when they actually constrain computation instead of merely decorating the narrative.
