OSCAR is the most directly relevant paper. It finetunes Cosmos-Predict2.5-2B as an action-conditioned video world model, using 2D kinematic skeleton renderings as a unified conditioning representation across four robot embodiments and human hands. The useful claim is not merely that generated robot videos look better. The useful claim is that a generated-world policy-evaluation protocol on RoboArena correlates with real-world policy ranking, while the skeleton condition gives a better generalization/precision trade-off than latent action or robot-specific mesh renders.

The quadrotor generalization study is less flashy and probably more important for keeping later world-model claims honest. It trains DreamerV3-style world models under different environmental-randomness regimes, evaluates cross-environment reconstruction during self-supervised pretraining and policy performance after RL, then deploys every model on a real quadrotor. The sharp result is that the model that looked best in simulation RL failed in the harder real deployment, while models with better cross-environment SSL reconstruction transferred.

A 3D Isovist World Model is adjacent rather than directly robotic manipulation work, but it has one of the cleaner representation arguments of the week. It predicts the geometry of open navigable volume as a spherical depth map, not RGB appearance or flattened occupancy. It also makes persistent spatial memory explicit through a writable latent BEV map. The empirical claims are still modest, especially the spatial-map ablation, but the representation is conceptually crisp.

Most relevant today: OSCAR.

The steal-worthy idea is the evaluation contract. OSCAR is not claiming that generated worlds are now perfect robot simulators. It narrows the job: given the first frame and a candidate action trajectory, generate a rollout good enough to compare policies against real RoboArena rankings. Skeleton conditioning matters because it says what the action is in a spatially aligned, texture-free, embodiment-flexible form. That is much healthier than hiding action in a latent and hoping the video model learns what motion means.

The quadrotor paper is nearly as important because it attacks a failure mode that will keep recurring: a world model can look good under the wrong validation signal. Its result suggests that self-supervised predictive generalization across environments may be a better deployment predictor than simulated RL success. That is a serious baseline-framing lesson.

OSCAR raises the bar for action-conditioned robot world models. It is no longer enough to show plausible videos. A serious paper should say how action is represented, whether that representation transfers across embodiments, and whether generated rollouts correlate with real policy outcomes.

The quadrotor paper raises the bar for sim-to-real world-model validation. If a paper reports strong simulation policy returns but never stress-tests predictive robustness under environment shift, its deployment claim should be treated as weak.

The isovist paper is not a baseline reset for robotics, but it is a framing correction. For navigation, the right predictive target may be the geometry of reachable open space rather than appearance. The paper's own evidence is still early, but the representation choice is cleaner than most "world model" branding.

The best paper today is OSCAR because it makes generated robot worlds answer an evaluation question rather than just a visual-fidelity question. The most important cautionary paper is the quadrotor generalization study, which shows that the validation metric deciding model selection can matter more than the simulator leaderboard. The best adjacent representation paper is A 3D Isovist World Model, because it predicts the negative space an agent moves through and gives that geometry a persistent writable memory. The common lesson is simple: world models earn their name when their state or conditioning interface changes what can be tested, transferred, or trusted.
