Most relevant today: Scores Are Not Decisions: Cost-Aware Stopping for Tool Acquisition in LLM Agents.

The reason is blunt. Cabbageland keeps building agents in environments where exposing every plausible tool is not free. It burns context, latency, money, privacy surface, and error surface. This paper says the obvious thing that too many stacks still ignore: a ranking is only an ordering, not a deployment policy. Once costs differ, the right question is whether the next tool's marginal value clears its marginal cost.

The rest of the digest reinforces the same discipline from other angles. What Can Latent World Models Know? says representation claims should be target-gated, not narrated from vibes. Visual Credit Audit says benchmark wins should be evidence-qualified, not accepted at face value. Prior Directions says persistent context can become a structured override channel rather than a harmless reminder. Cost-Sensitive Conformal Prediction says uncertainty guarantees are only meaningful if they protect the cases that actually matter.

Scores Are Not Decisions is strongest because it reframes tool acquisition as a stopping problem over ranked prefixes instead of a thresholding problem over raw scores. The important baseline lesson is that score-only rules become structurally wrong once costs are heterogeneous.

What Can Latent World Models Know? is strongest because it separates recoverability from retention. The useful novelty is the certificate-gated protocol: first prove a parameter is present in the observations, then ask whether the objective made the latent keep it.

Visual Credit Audit is strongest because it changes what benchmark accuracy is allowed to mean. Correctness without additional image support is not the same thing as multimodal reasoning, and the paper gives a post-hoc audit that makes that visible.

Prior Directions is strongest because it resists the lazy explanation that stale priors win simply by producing "more" internal movement. The paper's claim is tighter: stronger lock-in can come with smaller movement if that movement is organized along a compact recurrent route.

Cost-Sensitive Conformal Prediction is strongest as a deployment benchmark rather than as a deep new algorithmic idea. The useful lesson is that minority-class coverage, abstention policy, and human-review cost should be treated as one operational object rather than three separate dashboards.

The common lesson today is that the object you score is not necessarily the object you control. Tool relevance scores are not acquisition decisions. Fused sensory channels are not retained state. Benchmark accuracy is not image-grounded evidence use. Persistent priors are not benign context. Marginal coverage is not protection for the cases you most care about. The papers worth keeping are the ones that force the operational object to match the claim: the right tool prefix, the right latent target, the right evidence audit, the right intervention direction, the right uncertainty contract.
