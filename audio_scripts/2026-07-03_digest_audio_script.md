Maven is the most directly useful paper today. It replaces final-answer-only long-context RL with rewards over add / drop / link / answer actions in an editable evidence memory.

HOLA is the strongest architecture paper. Its useful move is small but sharp: let the recurrent state keep compressible structure, and reserve a bounded exact cache for high-residual associations that the state is likely to forget.

Is One Layer Enough? is the strongest post-training diagnostic. It argues that RL gains concentrate in a small, stable set of middle transformer layers across model families, tasks, and RL algorithms.

Most relevant today: Evidence-State Rewards for Long-Context Reasoning.

The steal is the reward interface. For a long-context or tool-using agent, the useful object is not just the final answer. It is the evolving state of evidence, plans, constraints, dropped distractors, and linked facts. Maven's add / drop / link / answer vocabulary is easy to map onto OpenClaw-style traces: tool outputs can enter a working evidence state, facts can be linked to claims, misleading context can be explicitly retired, and the agent can be rewarded for making the state more answer-supportive rather than merely lucky.

HOLA gives the architectural analogue: do not force exact episodic associations through a lossy recurrent bottleneck when a small exact store can carry the residues. The single-layer RL paper gives the training analogue: before updating everything, locate the layers or modules where adaptation actually changes behavior. Hidden Forgetting gives the evaluation analogue: if the model still answers correctly but the evidence path changed, something important was forgotten.

Maven is strong because it treats evidence navigation as the training target. The method defines an answer-conditioned evidence-state value using a frozen verifier's answer likelihood, then assigns action-level rewards to add, link, drop, and answer spans inside GRPO. Add actions receive marginal and hindsight credit, link actions receive synergy credit, and drop actions are rewarded when removing misleading evidence improves answer support. The paper reports gains over outcome-only RL and evidence-identification baselines on LongBench v2, LongReason, and RULER with Llama and Qwen backbones. The caveat is that the reward quality depends on the frozen verifier and on a structured action grammar; messy open-agent traces will need more robust state extraction.

HOLA is strong because the mechanism matches the failure. Delta-rule linear attention compresses many prefix associations into a fixed recurrent state; exact facts disappear under interference. HOLA keeps the normal compressive state but adds a bounded exact KV memory, writes entries selected by beta times residual norm, and reads them through a decoupled RMSNorm-gamma pathway. At 340M parameters trained on 15B SlimPajama tokens, the paper reports Wikitext perplexity improving from 27.32 to 22.92 and better RULER needle recall out to 32k tokens. The caveat is scale and breadth: 340M is a serious systems result, but not proof that the same simple cache policy remains optimal in larger frontier architectures.

Is One Layer Enough? is strong because it turns a vague RL-post-training story into a localization experiment. The layer-contribution metric compares single-layer RL improvement against full-parameter RL improvement. The headline is striking: high-contribution layers are concentrated in the middle of the transformer stack, and rankings are strongly correlated across datasets, model families, and algorithms. The caveat is that this is a controlled post-training study, not a deployment recipe yet. It tells us where adaptation appears to concentrate, not why those layers are sufficient or how the result interacts with safety alignment, preference drift, or very large models.

Hidden Forgetting is strong because it separates answer retention from evidence-path retention. The paper defines hidden evidence-use forgetting, estimates evidence-reliance profiles with counterfactual channel interventions, and trains RCL against task loss, prediction-preservation loss, and reliance-preservation loss. Its multimodal streams cover image-text, chart, OCR, document, and evidence-sensitive setups. The caveat is that reliance profiles are intervention-derived proxies, not direct causal proofs of internal reasoning; still, they are a better signal than accuracy alone.

Online Safety Monitoring is useful because it keeps the monitor's decision contract simple and explicit. The monitor thresholds an external verifier signal, calibrates the threshold with conformal / risk-control style methods, and evaluates on mathematical reasoning and red-teaming data. The caveat is that it inherits the verifier's blind spots; risk control over a weak verifier is not safety, just calibrated ignorance.

WorldDirector was the strongest 3D / generative-media runner-up. It decouples semantic motion orchestration from visual generation by using an LLM to coordinate 3D trajectories and camera motion, then feeding those trajectories as video-generation controls so objects can leave view and re-enter with stable identity. I did not preserve it today because the top five had broader model / agent mechanisms, but it is worth revisiting for persistent dynamic memory in video world simulators.

A rubric-based controlled comparison of frontier language models on expert-authored clinical reasoning tasks was the best medical runner-up. It is tiny - five cases - but the method is good: clinician-authored open-ended tasks with weighted atomic rubrics. The most useful finding is the priority inversion: models satisfy many low-stakes criteria while missing a large fraction of critical criteria. I kept it as a runner-up because the dataset is preliminary rather than a mature benchmark.

Safety Testing LLM Agents at Scale, Bayesian Sparse LoRA for Uncertainty Estimation, ContextNest, and WorldDirector were worthwhile scouting hits. None beat the top four on mechanism reuse today.

The best papers today all attack hidden state with local structure. Maven says long-context agents should be trained on evidence-state transitions. HOLA says efficient sequence models need a small exact store for associations their recurrent state overwrites. Single-layer RL says post-training gains live in identifiable middle-layer loci rather than uniformly across the stack. Hidden Forgetting says accuracy can survive while grounding dies. Online Safety says deployment monitors need a calibrated risk contract. Same lesson from five angles: a system is only as trustworthy as the intermediate state it exposes, preserves, and optimizes.
