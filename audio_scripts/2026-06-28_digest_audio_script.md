Decision-Aligned Evaluation of Uncertainty Quantification is the most relevant paper today. It gives a decision-theoretic way to ask whether an uncertainty metric actually ranks models by downstream usefulness, then shows why many standard scores are misaligned or secretly encode strange priors.

PhysiFormer is the strongest world-model mechanism. It predicts full 3D mesh trajectories directly in world coordinates instead of doing view-bound video prediction or autoregressive coordinate rollout.

Most relevant today: Decision-Aligned UQ.

The steal is the metric audit. If a score is used to decide whether an agent should act, ask for help, route to another model, retrieve more evidence, or abstain, the score should declare which decision family it serves. "Good calibration" is not the same thing as "useful for this decision."

PhysiFormer gives the representation version of the same lesson. If the goal is physical reasoning, use a state space where geometry and motion are first-class. Beyond the Hard Budget gives the feature-dictionary version: sparse does not automatically mean clean, and the regularizer has to target the part of the code where the failure lives.

Decision-Aligned UQ is strong because it gives a formal test for whether an uncertainty metric preserves expected downstream utility under a prior over decision parameters. The paper then uses that test to show that common metrics are often not aligned with common decision families or are aligned only under pathological priors. Its proposed prior-weighted utility metrics are proper scoring rules by construction and are empirically better aligned with controlled utilities and applied economic case studies. The caveat is that prior elicitation becomes part of evaluation, and no single PWU metric can cover all deployment objectives.

PhysiFormer is strong because it changes the modeling substrate. It predicts full future vertex trajectories in world coordinates, conditioned on initial positions, velocities, and material type, using a diffusion transformer with spatial, object-level, and temporal attention. On the 10k rigid dataset, it beats autoregressive baselines on long-horizon MSE and rigidity loss, and the paper shows qualitative generalization to unseen geometries, more objects, and mixed materials. The caveat is serious: it is still simulator-trained, 49-frame, bounded-environment dynamics with best performance around the tested mesh scale, and the authors report spurious contacts, interpenetration, and rare orientation discontinuities.

Beyond the Hard Budget is strong because it names a subtle Top-k SAE failure: the model can satisfy the k budget while leaving noisy off-support activations and brittle dependence on the training k. The off-support l1 regularizer improves monosemanticity and class purity most; the l1/l2 ratio regularizer concentrates information and improves robustness to inference-time k changes. The active-unit mask ablation is the key evidence: without the mask, regularization kills many more units. The caveat is scope: vision embeddings, proxy interpretability metrics, and no causal steering tests.

TAVR-VLM was the strongest healthcare runner-up. Its useful mechanism is a "risk -> region -> word" grounding path: predict procedural risk, use it to select risk-relevant anatomical evidence, and constrain clinically salient token grounding inside that support. The paper reports lower hallucination and better mIoU on a 1,482-patient multimodal TAVR cohort. I did not preserve it as a full note because the dataset is private/new, external validation is future work, and the evidence is more domain-system packaging than broadly reusable evaluation or representation mechanism.
