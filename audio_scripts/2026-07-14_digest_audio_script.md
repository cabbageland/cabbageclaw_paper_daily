Most relevant today: Can a Language Model Learn Facts Continually in Its Weights?

The main steal is the separation between storage and access. A model can keep the log-probability trace of an earlier fact and still fail to retrieve it once later writes redirect the question pathways that used to reach it. That is the right warning for any agent stack tempted to treat fine-tuning as durable memory. If a fact must be composed, survive later updates, and remain queryable on demand, the paper's conclusion is severe but useful: weights are not the system of record.

Think Through a Bottleneck gives the complementary pipeline lesson: if reasoning stages leak too much latent sludge across boundaries, self-refinement does not stay anchored to the rule. Inside the Unfair Judge supplies the evaluator lesson: if a judge can be displaced along low-dimensional bias directions, then evaluation stacks need judge-health checks rather than faith. HASTE adds the deployment lesson: when distribution shift is real, per-scene adaptation and operator-facing simplicity can beat bigger offline assumptions.

Can a Language Model Learn Facts Continually in Its Weights? is strong because it refuses to score continual learning only by whether a model can still recite a fact after writing it. The real distinction is between knowledge creation, long-horizon reachability, and general capability preservation. Broad study-style data narrows the recitation-to-use gap and improves later retention, but no tested intervention keeps earlier facts reliably reachable after many subsequent writes. The paper's framing, "stored but question-keyed," is the useful part. Caveat: the study is mostly on Qwen3 with invented facts and mostly LoRA writes, so this is a sharp boundary probe rather than the final word on all continual learning.

Inside the Unfair Judge is strong because it turns bias into something measurable in representation space rather than leaving it as prompt folklore. Geometry, causal steering, and failure prediction all point at the same hidden-state story. The cross-domain detector result matters because it survives unseen benchmarks instead of only working on the papers' own perturbation templates. Caveat: the activation-level claims depend on white-box access to three open-source judges, and controlled bias constructions are not the whole space of real evaluator failure.

Think Through a Bottleneck is strong because it isolates the causal variable that many multi-agent or structured-reasoning papers blur: not role labels, but context boundaries. The ablations are the best part. Remove physical isolation and the structured intermediate can actively hurt. Caveat: the bottleneck is still soft and prompt-enforced rather than architectural, the method is expensive in calls and tokens, and the current task suite is tilted toward deterministic rule induction rather than messy probabilistic reasoning.

HASTE is strong because it documents a system that has to work under the actual constraints the benchmark culture prefers to ignore. The key move is not a new giant model, but a pair of fast, operator-facing adaptation routes that assume only post-event imagery and a small labeling budget. Caveat: the xBD experiments are preliminary, binary, and cleaner than real disaster imagery, while the live system still misses damage invisible from overhead and inherits footprint registration problems.

Read It Back is strong because it extracts a reward directly from image-conditioned prompt recovery rather than from extra annotation or hand-built verification scaffolds. The self-reward case for unified multimodal models is especially interesting because it says alignment between reward source and generator may matter more than brute reward-model scale. Caveat: this is still a text-to-image RL paper with limited direct evidence about human preference, safety, or cross-domain reward robustness.

ZendoWorld was the closest runner-up. I like the benchmark because it tests hypothesis formation plus experiment design instead of passive pattern matching, and it clearly shows current VLM agents propose bad experiments. I left it out because today's final five had sharper direct design lessons. LaGuadia was another good runner-up: clinical language as a semantic anchor for multi-teacher pathology distillation is a real idea, but the deployment and transfer story felt narrower than the papers above.

The best papers today all reject the idea that storing more stuff is the same as making a system more capable. Weight writes do not guarantee later access. Judge outputs do not explain judge bias unless you inspect the activation routes underneath them. Self-refinement does not become rigorous until the boundary between stages is physically enforced. Reward models do not need to be separate critics if prompt recoverability already exposes the right alignment signal. Fielded damage-assessment systems do not need huge global assumptions if they can adapt locally with a small operator loop. Same lesson in five costumes: the important part is not just where the information lives, but what interface still lets the system use it correctly later.
