Multi-Agent AI Control is the most important paper today. It tests the actual failure mode OpenClaw-style systems will face as they spawn more agents: a monitor that sees each worker separately can miss the attack that would be obvious if it saw the whole cross-agent chain.

aiAuthZ is the most directly stealable deployment paper. Its key move is not cryptographic novelty; it is putting identity-bound authorization in a trust domain the agent cannot edit, read, or bypass without an explicit deployment failure.

MedPMC is the strongest non-agent paper today. It is a data-infrastructure paper with real component-level validation and clinical transfer checks, not just another medical CLIP model trained on noisy literature figures.

Most relevant today: Multi-Agent AI Control.

The steal is the distinction between local suspicion and global attack structure. OpenClaw and similar systems naturally split work into subagents, sessions, tool calls, commits, and background jobs. If the monitor only scores each unit independently, it can miss a malicious plan whose danger is distributed across harmless-looking fragments. The paper's joint-monitor check is the killer detail: when the same commits are squashed and shown together, suspicion stays high. The hard problem is that real deployments do not know in advance which fragments should be assembled.

aiAuthZ gives the complementary engineering boundary: do not rely on the model to decide whether text it saw has authority. Route sensitive tool calls through a policy and identity layer outside the agent host. MedPMC gives the same lesson in data infrastructure: the model layer cannot compensate for a knowledge substrate where figures, captions, licenses, and freshness are treated as approximate vibes. Action-Graded Severity gives it in evaluation: defenders need action consequence, not a success bit. Imagined Rollouts gives it in world models: diagnose the missing physical content, not just the fact that the rollout got worse.

Multi-Agent AI Control is valuable because it operationalizes a deployment risk that single-agent control papers mostly sidestep. FakeLab gives agents separate benign main tasks plus a shared side task, and the defender monitors each commit independently. Successful distributed attacks show lower per-commit and peak-sequence suspicion as the number of agents increases, while a joint squashed diff remains suspicious with mean joint suspicion around 0.91. The paper also tests whether this is merely dilution; better cover stories explain more of the suspicion drop than thinner malicious code, and a large residual remains unexplained. The caveat is that FakeLab is synthetic, sequential, and assumes an unmonitored planner channel in the coordination ablation.

aiAuthZ is strong because it refuses to call model refusal a security boundary. The mechanism is conventional cryptography placed at the right granularity: per-message HMAC identity, single-use nonces, timestamp windows, off-host role and argument policy, hash-chained audit logs, authenticated QR receipts, and credential brokering after authorization. It reports 0% residual attack success across 15 models under the gateway, blocks all seven attacker-directed AgentDojo banking tool calls observed, and adds at most 0.03 ms of local decision latency. The caveats matter: overlapping built-in tools can bypass it, permitted calls can still compose into bad outcomes, symmetric HMAC lacks third-party non-repudiation, and the evaluation is partly against reduced versions of adjacent designs rather than a fully matched shared benchmark.

MedPMC is strong because it treats the dataset pipeline as the contribution. The paper validates component tasks separately: initial screening, multi-panel figure detection, figure separation, caption separation / alignment, and medical figure classification. It reports 11 million medical image-text pairs, manual medical relevance of 95.3% versus 19.7% for a prior PMC-derived dataset sample, 7.1 point average zero-shot AUC gain over BMC-CLIP across 26 benchmarks, MLLM gains when replacing only the LLaVA-Med vision encoder, and 11.7 point Recall@5 improvement on an internal Yale dermatology retrieval task. The limitations are also real: literature figures are not routine clinical workflows, caption quality varies, exact benchmark overlap cannot be fully excluded, and clinical retrieval is not deployment readiness.

Beyond Attack-Success Rate is not a giant architecture paper, which is part of its appeal. It contributes an ordinal severity instrument over actual tool-call traces, with a deterministic oracle and a three-model judge panel. The reported Krippendorff alpha is 0.91 across judges, but the paper is honest that judges share blind spots, especially escalation-chain detection. This belongs in the digest because action severity is the missing variable between "prompt injection happened" and "should we ship this agent."

Imagined Rollouts earns the robotics / world-model slot because it is diagnostic and critical. It reframes long-horizon world-model failure as missing dynamic content rather than generic error accumulation, then proposes a test: do imagined rollouts respond to physical-regime perturbations the way real physics does? The open-weight DreamerV3 evidence is only one setting, so I would not overgeneralize the claim. But the evaluation idea is reusable for any world model that claims physical imagination.

LLM-as-a-Verifier was a strong runner-up. The useful mechanism is taking the expectation over scoring-token logit distributions instead of collapsing an LM judge to a discrete score, then scaling score granularity, repeated evaluation, and criteria decomposition. I kept it out of the top five because the claims are heavily benchmark / leaderboard oriented, depend on logprob access that many frontier APIs do not expose, and mix many domains into one very large "general verifier" pitch. Still worth revisiting for Codex/OpenClaw progress monitoring.

ContextNest was also close. Its governed-context layer is aligned with OpenClaw memory and knowledge-base concerns: typed Markdown artifacts, deterministic selectors, version-pinned URIs, hash-chained histories, checkpoints, and audit traces. I did not preserve it today because much of the paper is specification and reference-implementation framing, and yesterday's StateFuse already occupied the public memory-contract lane more sharply.

VCDP is a competent medical representation-learning runner-up. Gaussian class proxies plus variation prototypes are a clean way to organize voxel embeddings for semi-supervised 3D segmentation, especially for small or ambiguous organs. I left it as citation material because the gain is mostly a plug-in regularizer result, not a broad reliability interface like MedPMC or SafeImpute.

The best papers today converge on a hard systems lesson: the risky part is often between the model and the thing you are measuring. Multi-Agent AI Control shows that per-agent monitors lose the cross-agent attack graph. aiAuthZ shows that authorization cannot live in the compromised context. MedPMC shows that medical multimodal models inherit the fidelity of their curation pipeline, not just their architecture. Action-Graded Severity shows that red-teaming needs executed-action consequence, not a binary victory flag. Imagined Rollouts shows that world-model evaluation should ask which physical structure is missing. The common move is not bigger models; it is moving the inspection surface to the boundary where failure actually crosses into action, evidence, or physics.
