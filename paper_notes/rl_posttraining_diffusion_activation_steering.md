# RL Post-Training in Diffusion Transformers Is Activation Steering

## Basic info

* Title: RL Post-Training in Diffusion Transformers Is Activation Steering
* Authors: Tuna Han Salih Meral, Kaan Oktay, Alec Helbling, Atakan Kara, Duen Horng Chau, Pinar Yanardag
* Year: 2026
* Venue / source: arXiv preprint, submitted November 19, 2026; version 1 inspected
* Link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.26627
* Date surfaced: 2026-11-21
* Why selected in one sentence: A deliberately input-independent intervention recovers most measured RL gains, supplying a strong control for claims that post-training learns new prompt-dependent computation.

## Quick verdict

* Highly relevant

Preserve the restricted-interface experiment and its causal controls. The evidence supports constant interventions being sufficient for most measured reward improvement in the tested policy-gradient checkpoints; it does not establish that the original RL update is literally constant, uniquely explain its mechanism, or prove that RL cannot learn new capabilities. The title is broader than the defensible conclusion.

## One-paragraph overview

Take an RL-tuned image or video generator and its original base. Freeze the base and add a small collection of learned vectors at transformer-block outputs. A vector is shared across every token, prompt, and sampling seed, though different blocks, streams, guidance passes, and timestep bins get different vectors. Fit those vectors to reproduce the tuned model's output velocity, rather than average its internal activation changes. This recovers most reward improvement across six image and two selected video checkpoints. A separate vector-only RL experiment shows that the interface can learn directly from reward, while attention-route replacements test how its constant changes become prompt-specific. Supervised subject-identity learning supplies a useful boundary: input-dependent adapters preserve details the constant interface misses.

## Model definition

### Inputs

The frozen diffusion/flow transformer receives a noisy image/video latent, flow timestep, and prompt conditioning; the frame-conditioned video setting also receives its first frame. SD3.5-M uses separate conditional and empty-prompt classifier-free guidance passes. The fitted vectors receive no prompt, token content, or random seed: their indices are only block/output location, image/text stream when applicable, guidance pass, and one of two fixed schedule bins. SD3.5-M uses 40 sampling steps, with the default early/late split at flow time 0.75 on its shifted schedule.

### Outputs

The generator predicts a latent velocity for integration into an image or video. At each selected hidden output, the intervention adds the same vector to every token: `h_new = h + alpha * s[block, pass, bin, stream]`, with alpha equal to one in the main evaluation. The adaptation parameters are vectors, not new per-prompt activations generated by a separate controller.

### Training objective (loss)

**Checkpoint distillation:** Minimize expected squared Euclidean error between steered-base velocity and RL-teacher velocity on the same noisy state, timestep, and conditioning. Build states by taking final teacher-generated samples and re-noising them; this approximates relevant sampling states without rolling out during each fitting update. The expectation samples prompts, noise, timestep, and guidance pass. Both models' original weights remain frozen.

The fit normally starts from sequentially measured mean-difference vectors, but zero initialization gives similar gains in selected OCR/PickScore controls. Main fitting uses AdamW, no weight decay, and gradient clipping. The regression loss contains no reward; selection among fitting checkpoints uses validation reward. A loss-only selection control produces similar results within the reported uncertainty on the tested subsets. Thus “no reward in the objective” is accurate, but “no reward used anywhere” is not.

**Direct RL:** Separately initialize the vectors at zero and run Flow-GRPO on OCR or PickScore reward with the base frozen. This is online reward optimization with generation/rollout cost, not velocity distillation or an inference-only procedure.

### Architecture / parameterization

Output-additive activation steering inside pretrained flow/diffusion transformers. On SD3.5-M, 47 image/text output locations, width 1,536, two guidance passes, and two time bins give 188 vectors and 288,768 parameters. The reference rank-32 RL LoRA has 18.8M parameters, about 65 times as many. Other architectures adjust output locations, stream sharing, and guidance-pass indexing. The intervention is input-independent; the frozen transformer's response to it is not.

## Key questions this summary must address

### 1. What problem is the paper trying to solve?

Does the measured improvement from diffusion-model RL post-training require new input-dependent transformations, or can a much simpler interface steer computation the base already supports? Prompt-specific output changes do not by themselves answer that question: a constant bias inside a nonlinear conditional model can have prompt-specific consequences.

### 2. What is the method?

1. Collect teacher samples on training prompts and query teacher/base activations.
2. Establish a mean-difference steering baseline, indexed by the same locations and sampler metadata as the learned vectors.
3. Fit vectors to final velocity predictions on re-noised teacher samples. Unlike local averaging, this optimizes the downstream effect through the entire remaining frozen computation.
4. Evaluate matched prompts and initial noise against base and RL teacher, reporting raw reward gains and recovered fractions with paired bootstrap intervals.
5. Compare a parameter-matched input-dependent LoRA trained with the same velocity objective and update budget.
6. Run vector-only Flow-GRPO from zero on two SD3.5-M rewards.
7. Restrict vectors to particular streams/passes/time bins and replace selected attention reads with unsteered-base keys/values.

There is no symbolic planner, explicit environment state, or memory module. The useful structure is an intervention interface and controlled access to existing computation.

### 3. What is the method motivation?

A matrix update can respond differently to each token, while a shared additive vector cannot introduce that new input dependence at its insertion point. If the latter nevertheless achieves most reward gains, the benchmark does not require the full expressive freedom of the weight update. Velocity matching makes this a serious sufficiency test rather than an unfairly weak mean-activation approximation.

### 4. What data does it use?

Public base and RL-tuned checkpoints spanning SD3.5-M, FLUX.1-dev, Wan2.1-1.3B, and the VBVR-Pro setting based on Wan2.2-5B. Rewards include PickScore, OCR, GenEval, HPSv2.1/HPSv3, and a rule-scored video-reasoning setting. Teacher samples come from the checkpoints' training-prompt sources; test prompts/instances are held out under each stated protocol.

SD3.5-M fitting uses 10,000 prompts; other fits use hundreds to a few thousand. Main test sizes include 2,048 PickScore prompts with one sampling seed, 1,018 OCR prompts with one seed, 553 GenEval prompts with four seeds, 256 Wan2.1 prompts with two seeds, and 250 VBVR instances with four seeds. Some targeted controls use smaller subsets. These are benchmark/source-conditioned tests, not unrestricted distribution shift.

### 5. How is it evaluated?

The recovered share is `(steered reward - base reward) / (RL reward - base reward)`. Report raw differences alongside it: the ratio can exceed 100% and its uncertainty depends on the denominator. The paper uses paired bootstrap intervals, other reward scorers, diversity/color statistics, changed sampling settings/resolutions, same-size adapters, and a human comparison over 50 prompts and 25 raters.

On SD3.5-M, route interventions replace one category of joint-attention key/value reads with the base's corresponding reads on the same noisy state. These are stronger evidence than attention maps, but are still network interventions rather than an identification of the unique natural computation.

### 6. What are the main results?

- **SD3.5-M PickScore:** fitted shift gains 1.416 reward points versus teacher's 1.560; 90.8% recovery, 95% interval 89.8–91.7%.
- **SD3.5-M OCR:** 0.325 versus 0.344; 94.4%, interval 90.8–97.9%. Mean-difference steering recovers only 21.5%.
- **SD3.5-M GenEval:** 0.260 versus 0.271; 95.9%, interval 92.0–99.7%. Mean-difference steering recovers only 12.2%.
- **Across image checkpoints:** 90.0–96.3% recovery, including LoRA and full-weight RL updates.
- **Selected video checkpoints:** Wan2.1 gives 111.9% recovery, interval 106.5–118.0%; VBVR gives 89.8%, interval 76.2–105.6%. The latter is not a precise estimate of near-complete recovery.
- **Direct vector RL:** PickScore reaches 103.6% and OCR 104.6% of the reference RL gains, demonstrating learnability through the restricted interface on these two settings.
- **Routing:** resetting what image queries read from text leaves 10.5% of fitted PickScore gain and 20.5% of OCR gain; analogous interventions leave 24.5% and 26.4% of the original RL models' gains.
- **Restriction:** conditional-pass, early-bin, text-stream vectors together use 35,328 parameters and recover 89.5% of OCR and 73.7% of PickScore gain. A smaller interface remains useful, but not equally strong on every reward.

The human study prefers steered over base images and finds no significant difference versus RL-tuned images. That is not an equivalence test. Same-size fitted LoRA shows no detected advantage in the main RL comparisons, with steering better on the selected Wan2.1 evaluation; supervised DreamBooth identity fitting reverses the story, favoring LoRA's fine detail.

### 7. What is actually novel?

Not activation steering, parameter-efficient adaptation, or the idea that pretrained models already contain useful behavior. The substantive combination is output-velocity fitting as a deliberately constant probe across several policy-gradient diffusion checkpoints, direct reward optimization of that same interface, and route-level controls linking it to the tuned models' text-to-image information path. The conclusion should remain about the expressive requirements of measured improvements, not an ontological verdict on RL.

### 8. What are the strengths?

The restriction is legible and falsifiable. A weak averaging baseline is separated from an optimized intervention. Matched-size adapters address a straightforward parameter-budget objection; direct RL addresses the objection that the vectors merely inherit an expensive teacher's discovery. Side-effect measurements and the supervised counterexample prevent a purely celebratory compression story. The attention analysis also explains a real mechanism: in joint attention, a shared text-key shift changes text-versus-image attention odds by a query-dependent factor, and a shared value shift has an effect weighted by each query's text attention mass.

### 9. What are the weaknesses, limitations, or red flags?

**Sufficiency is not identity.** Many parameterizations can reproduce a scalar reward. The original weight update can still implement input-dependent changes the compressed surrogate omits. Distillation demonstrates an alternative solution, not the unique causal explanation of every teacher behavior.

**The scope is selected policy-gradient checkpoints.** Preference optimization and differentiable reward backpropagation remain untested. Of six screened video RL models, only two with clearly positive gains on evaluated scores enter the main recovery comparison. This selection is disclosed, but narrows generality.

**Reward recovery does not preserve a distribution.** PickScore steering inherits the tuned model's diversity reduction: within-prompt diversity is about 0.522 of base versus the teacher's 0.517, and color energy about 0.542 versus 0.550. Successful reward optimization can include undesirable shifts. Other-scorer agreement helps, but does not replace broad perceptual and failure testing.

**Small parameters do not mean free training.** Teacher sampling, mean estimation, and fitting cost up to roughly 17 H100 GPU-hours per image model and 70 per video model. Direct vector-only RL uses 123 B200 GPU-hours for PickScore and 183 for OCR. These hardware figures are not directly interchangeable, and vector count alone is not a compute comparison with full RL.

**Mechanistic interventions need care.** Replacing attention reads may create off-manifold states and disrupt cooperating routes. Similar necessity patterns are useful evidence, not proof that two networks implement identical algorithms. The simple attention identity is exact for its stated shared post-normalization key/value assumptions; actual normalized shifts can differ across tokens, requiring a first-order interpretation.

**Uncertainty is uneven.** Most fits use one training seed; selected OCR/PickScore fits have three-seed controls. Many main rewards use one generation seed, and the human comparison is small. A confidence interval including zero for a difference does not prove equal performance.

### 10. What challenges or open problems remain?

Find tasks for which no fixed-vector interface can recover gains despite a well-optimized fit. Separate interpolation within training-prompt sources from transfer to genuinely novel layouts, languages, identities, and long temporal dependencies. Determine whether the missing input-dependent computation matters for robustness or rare failures even when average reward is matched. Distinguish limitations of current RL algorithms from limitations of the reward/task itself.

### 11. What future work naturally follows?

Optimize constant, gated, low-rank, and full-update interfaces under matched rollout and tuning budgets. Add reward-free checkpoint selection throughout, multiple training seeds, and human comparisons designed for equivalence or noninferiority rather than merely failing to reject a difference. Evaluate cross-reward composition and interference: two individually useful constant shifts may not combine cleanly. Test intervention-route conclusions with complementary patching and matched perturbation controls. Use supervised identity/detail transfer as a positive control that the test can detect when input-dependent adaptation helps.

### 12. Why does this matter for cabbageland?

It supplies a concrete baseline for mechanistic claims about learned controllability. A new capability claim should survive a simple intervention at the right interface before being credited to new modular computation. This transfers to world models and policy adaptation as an experimental question, not an established result in those domains. It also warns against confusing a low-dimensional control interface with an interpretable internal program.

### 13. What ideas are steal-worthy?

- Test a tightly constrained interface before enlarging an adaptation module.
- Match the downstream predictive object, not just a local mean activation difference.
- Separate teacher imitation, direct reward learning, and causal routing into different experiments.
- Index control by explicit sampler metadata when effects differ by guidance pass or stage.
- Use both negative controls that should fail and positive counterexamples that expose the interface's limits.
- Track inherited harms, diversity, and total training cost alongside reward recovery.

### 14. Final decision

Preserve as a mechanism/evaluation note and a high-priority baseline recipe. Do not cite it as proof that all diffusion RL is “just steering,” that novel skills are impossible, or that fewer trainable parameters automatically make training cheap.
